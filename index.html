How to read it

Scroll the way you normally do. People roll a wheel in finger strokes: two to seven notches, a pause while the finger comes back, then the next stroke. The tester splits your rolling into those strokes, drawn as solid runs along the centre line with the notches in each, and judges every wrong-way event by where it falls in a stroke.

Reversed is an event against the roll in the middle of a stroke that flipped within 60 ms; a false start is the same thing just before a stroke gets going. A finger cannot turn a wheel back that fast, so it is the wheel: usually dirty or worn encoder contacts. Bounce-back is a wrong-way event straight after the last notch of a stroke, as the wheel settles or the finger lifts. Back-step is one slow event against the roll in the pause between two strokes. That can be the wheel, or your finger dragging it back on the way to the next stroke, so it counts as suspect: lift your finger clear between strokes and see if they stop.

Coverage shows how much rolling each direction has had at each speed: single notches, slow rolls, normal strokes and fast flicks. Faults often show at one speed only, so the verdict stays at "clean so far" until every cell has had enough. Slow, steady rolling exposes the most: the browser hands over wheel events once per screen frame and adds up what arrived in between, so in a fast flick a stray can cancel against a real notch.

Checks need you to take part. Notches per turn: mark the wheel, roll whole turns, and the count per turn falls out. Most wheels have 24 notches, 15° apart; 18 is the other common count. Counted notches: roll exactly ten clicks by feel. If the tester saw nine or eleven, the wheel is dropping or doubling notches; dust in the wheel sensor is the usual cause of dropped ones. Hands off: ten seconds without touching the wheel. Anything it reports then, it is doing by itself.

The wheel map is one turn of the wheel. The hand shows where the wheel is now and each dot is a fault. Once the notches per turn are measured, dots piling up in one place suggest the fault sits at one spot on the wheel, which is worth knowing if you open the mouse to clean it.

Notch size is what your browser reports for one notch. A high-resolution wheel sends several events per notch and steps back a little as it settles; the tester spots that and stops calling those faults. If the sizes vary, you have a smooth wheel, a touchpad or OS acceleration, and the fault counts mean less. Wheel click counts presses of the wheel button; a double-fire is a second press under 50 ms after release, which points to a worn switch.

Before you blame the wheel: blow it out with compressed air, close the mouse maker's software or turn off its smooth scrolling, plug in directly instead of through a hub, and try another port or another computer. A fault that follows the mouse is the mouse. If faults stop while you press the wheel gently sideways, its axle is loose in the encoder.
